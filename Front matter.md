# Front matter

The small block at the very top of a note, between two lines of three dashes.

Scroll to the top of this note and you are looking at one.

## What it is

```
---
title: What actually makes our content land
type: note
tags: [content, second-brain]
created: 2026-08-05
status: raw
---
```

That is a note telling you what it is, before you read a word of it.

## What it is for

Not decoration. Finding.

Today you have a handful of notes and you know where every one of them is. In
two years you have four hundred and you have forgotten writing most of them.
Front matter is the bit of work your past self does so your future self can find
things without remembering them.

Try it now, in this folder. Click the tag `second-brain` where Obsidian shows
it, and every note carrying that tag comes back at once. No plugin, no setup, no
search terms.

## The five fields worth using

Keep it short. A long block that you stop filling in is worse than a short one
you always do.

| Field | What it does |
|---|---|
| `title` | What the note is called, in case the filename drifts |
| `type` | What kind of thing it is: a note, a person, a meeting, a source |
| `tags` | The one that does the finding. Two or three, not ten |
| `created` | When you wrote it. Useful more often than you would think |
| `status` | Where it is up to: raw, living, planned, done. Honest, not aspirational |

Add fields when you want to slice notes a new way. Do not add them because a
template told you to.

## The bit that pays off later

The block at the top is also the first thing an AI reads when you point one at
your folder. It is how it tells a meeting note from a source from a
half-finished idea without opening every single file.

So the ten seconds you spend on this is what makes [[CONNECT-AN-AI]] work
properly rather than adequately.
