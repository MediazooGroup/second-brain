# Differentiators

Five of them. Each one is written as the thing we say, followed by the objection it answers, because that is the order they get used in.

## 1. We have been doing this in production for over a year

**The objection it answers: "we can do this ourselves."**

They probably can, eventually. What they are actually buying is the year we already spent getting it wrong. We know which folder structures collapse at the second department, which sessions lose the room, what happens when a security team says no in week four, and what a delegate does with a skill on the Monday after the workshop when nobody is watching.

Say it plainly and without swagger: *"You could. We'd just be handing you the year we already spent finding out where this breaks."* Then offer the specific thing — the prioritisation, the structure, the measure — rather than arguing about capability. Nobody wins that argument.

## 2. We prioritise before we design

**The objection it answers: "we already know what we want to train people on."**

Clients arrive with a list of twenty things. We spend the first half-day working out which three are worth changing — high frequency, low system complexity, an owner who wants it. The other seventeen are still there, in a written order, which is often the most valuable page of the whole engagement.

We describe what prioritisation *does*. We do not hand over the framework itself in a proposal or on a stage; it is the thing being bought.

## 3. The measure is agreed on day one

**The objection it answers: "how will we know it worked?"**

The client names the number before we design a session, and we capture its baseline before the first cohort runs. Not satisfaction scores. Something the department already reports on: minutes per quote, days to respond, rework rate.

This is also the promise we are worst at keeping — [[3-Acme Corp/01_Strategy/okrs-2026|KR3.2]] sits at 41% against a target of 80%. The fix is that the baseline is now a named deliverable with a date on it.

## 4. We build with the delegates, not for them

**The objection it answers: "our people won't engage with it."**

They engage when it is their own work on the screen. Every session runs on the delegate's own files from the first hour. What they leave with is theirs, in their words, and it already works on one real job. Nothing we hand over is demo material.

## 5. We hand over the method

**The objection it answers: "we don't want to be dependent on a supplier."**

The client can run the next cohort without us, and we write down how. It reads like it costs us the follow-on work. In practice it is the single most common reason a client comes back for the next department, because we are the supplier who did not try to become load-bearing.

## What we do not claim

- We do not claim proprietary technology. We work in the client's tools.
- We do not claim a benchmark database or industry averages. We measure the client against themselves.
- We do not quote outcomes we have not measured, ever. One published outcome story means we say "one", not "many".
