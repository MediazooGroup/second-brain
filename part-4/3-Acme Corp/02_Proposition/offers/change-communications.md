# Change communications

The smallest of the three offers by revenue, and the one that most often protects the other two.

## The problem it answers

The change is real, the programme is running, and the other four thousand people in the organisation have heard one all-staff email about it. In the absence of a story, people write their own — usually the least generous one available.

## What the client gets

- The change written as something a person would actually say, not as a set of bullet points from a steering pack.
- A four to eight week campaign: what lands, where, in what order, and who says it.
- Assets made from the client's own people — the delegates from the programme, on camera, saying what changed for them.
- A cadence the client's internal comms team can keep running after we go.

## How it runs

Campaign-shaped rather than cohort-shaped. Weeks one and two are listening and writing; weeks three onwards are publishing. The strongest material is always the delegate story, which is why this offer works best running one phase behind an AI adoption programme rather than alongside it.

## Why we keep it

- It is the cheapest way into a department that has not bought anything yet, because comms teams talk to everybody.
- It is how a proven outcome in one department becomes visible to the rest of the organisation, which is exactly the O2 bottleneck named in [[3-Acme Corp/01_Strategy/okrs-2026|the OKRs]].
- Our own [[4-Acme Marketing/INDEX|marketing department]] runs on the same material. A client outcome story told well internally is one edit away from being a published case study.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Written for the sponsor, not the audience.** If the sponsor is the only reviewer, the language drifts upward and lands nowhere.
- **Launched before there is anything true to say.** A campaign about a change that has not happened yet is the fastest way to burn trust.
- **Assets with no owner after we leave.** Agree who publishes week nine before we publish week one.
